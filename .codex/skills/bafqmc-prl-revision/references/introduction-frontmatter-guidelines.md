# Introduction And Frontmatter

Write for a broad physics readership. Connect the problem, its physical importance,
and the technical ideas clearly. The Introduction should give readers
a reason to care and a connected path to the result.

For BAFQMC, the distinction between local matrix-element positivity in WLQMC/SSE
and the global auxiliary-field weight provides a useful physical explanation of
the advance. Explain the meaning of symmetry and classification terminology as it
enters the argument. Let the current manuscript determine the exact scope.

State the contribution confidently and directly. Avoid unnecessary defensive
qualifications, repeated slogans, or specialist caveats that interrupt the
physical story. Preserve the conditions needed to understand a claim accurately.

Use prior work to establish the physical context and the scientific gap. Select
citations for the statements they support, and avoid turning the Introduction
into a list of methods, groups, or terminology. Adding a citation need not add
another sentence.

An abstract should convey the problem, central result, validation, and physical
opportunity. A conclusion should synthesize what the result makes possible.
Choose their structure from the actual argument rather than a fixed paragraph
count or checklist.

Figures and captions should remain intelligible to readers who scan them before
the body. Explain the objects, comparison, and intended inference without private
revision context.

Read the prose from front to back: each sentence should build on what readers
already know. Use familiar physics language, economical notation, and direct
positive sentences. Avoid negation-then-correction rhetoric and excessive colons,
semicolons, or dashes.
